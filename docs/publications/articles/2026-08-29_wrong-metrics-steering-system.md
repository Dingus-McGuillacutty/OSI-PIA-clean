# The Wrong Metrics Are Steering the System

## Publication Note

This article is part of the OSI-PIA public article series. It describes an active research and development direction. It does not claim that OSI-PIA is a finished hiring product, validated employment-screening system, legal compliance tool, or production decision system.

## Article

Hiring systems do not only fail because they lack information.

They fail because the wrong things are being measured.

Organizations measure applicant volume, time-to-fill, recruiter workload, cost per hire, credential match, keyword match, source channel, interview completion, assessment score, rejection rate, and process throughput.

Those metrics are not useless. Hiring is operational work. Volume is real. Workload is real. Risk is real. Organizations need ways to manage pipelines, compare applicants, document decisions, and avoid chaos.

But a hiring system can perform well on administrative metrics while failing at the deeper task.

It can process more applications while understanding fewer people. It can reduce recruiter workload while increasing false negatives. It can standardize evaluation while narrowing recognition. It can speed up rejection while losing access to capability.

It can appear efficient while becoming less capable of finding the people who can actually do the work.

That is the problem of wrong metrics.

## Capability Access Failure

Many organizations describe the current labor market as a talent-scarcity problem.

Sometimes that may be true in a narrow sense. Some roles require specific credentials, scarce technical experience, geographic availability, clearance, licensing, or difficult-to-find combinations of knowledge and judgment.

But as a broad explanation, “talent scarcity” is incomplete.

In many cases, the issue is not that capability is absent.

The issue is that organizations have lost access to capability.

Capable people may exist in the applicant pool, but the system fails to recognize them. They may be filtered out by rigid requirements, misread by automated systems, buried under volume, mismatched by job-title history, screened out by résumé formatting, ignored because their experience is nonlinear, or never routed to the person who could interpret their evidence.

That is not simple scarcity.

That is capability access failure.

The organization does not experience the candidate as a missed opportunity. It experiences the candidate as noise, mismatch, non-fit, or absence.

Then the organization reports that it cannot find people.

## Processing Is Not Recognition

The modern hiring system is often designed around intake and reduction.

A job opens. Applications arrive. The system parses, filters, sorts, ranks, flags, rejects, advances, and stores. The workflow exists to manage volume.

That is understandable.

But processing is not the same thing as recognition.

A system can process a résumé without understanding the person. It can extract a job title without interpreting the work. It can count years without understanding development. It can compare keywords without understanding context. It can reject a candidate without knowing what capability disappeared.

The Softchoice article on tech hiring describes the contradiction clearly: companies claim they cannot find IT talent while large numbers of experienced technology workers are displaced, applying repeatedly, and struggling to get through hiring systems.[^softchoice-brutal-normal]

That contradiction should make organizations pause.

If capable people are applying and companies still cannot see them, the problem may not be the talent supply alone.

It may be the recognition system.

## The Defensibility Trap

One of the strongest recent observations from recruiting commentary is that hiring has become less judgment-based and more defensibility-based.[^resilient-recruiter-normal]

Older models of hiring had their own failures: bias, inconsistency, favoritism, weak documentation, and unclear standards. But newer systems can fail differently.

Decision authority is now often distributed across applicant tracking systems, automated screens, HR policy, compliance requirements, risk controls, committees, hiring managers, recruiters, budget approvals, and vendor tools.

No single person may feel empowered to resolve uncertainty.

Instead of asking, “Who is best supported by the evidence?” the system can drift toward a safer procedural question:

**Which decision is easiest to defend?**

That shift matters.

A defensible decision is not always a wise decision. A consistent screen is not always a fair screen. A documented rejection is not always an accurate rejection.

A process can reduce variance while also reducing the organization’s ability to recognize unusual capability.

That is how hiring becomes procedurally safer and substantively weaker at the same time.

## The Metrics That Take Over

When organizations are overwhelmed, the measurable parts of the system become more powerful.

It is easy to measure how many applications arrived, how quickly they were screened, how many candidates were rejected, how many interviews were scheduled, how long the process took, which source produced applicants, how closely a résumé matched the posting, and whether a candidate met minimum criteria.

Those metrics can help manage workflow.

But they do not answer the deeper questions.

Did the system recognize real capability? Did it filter out people who could have done the work? Did the requirements measure the job or protect the process? Did the process reward polish over evidence? Did familiar career patterns get mistaken for qualification? Did cross-domain, nonlinear, self-taught, or hidden capability disappear before interpretation?

The danger is not measurement itself.

The danger is letting administrative metrics become substitutes for capability judgment.

## Speed Can Hide Loss

Speed is one of the most seductive metrics in hiring.

A faster process feels better than a slower one. Candidates want decisions. Recruiters want manageable queues. Hiring managers want roles filled. Executives want efficiency.

But speed can hide loss.

A fast rejection is not necessarily a good rejection. A fast screen is not necessarily a fair screen. A fast shortlist is not necessarily a strong shortlist.

If the system is filtering based on weak proxies, speed only accelerates misrecognition.

This is especially dangerous when the candidate’s strongest evidence is not already in the expected format: military experience, caregiving, self-directed learning, informal leadership, operational repair, project work, internal systems development, cross-functional translation, or nontraditional education.

A person with real capability may require interpretation.

The system may be optimized to avoid interpretation.

That is the conflict.

## Talent Scarcity or Development Failure?

There is another metric problem underneath the hiring problem.

Organizations often say they need experienced people, but many are reluctant to create the conditions under which experience develops.

The Softchoice article’s discussion of entry-level tech workers points toward this deeper issue: if companies stop hiring junior developers, where will future senior developers come from?[^softchoice-brutal-normal]

That question matters far beyond technology.

A labor market cannot produce senior capability if organizations refuse to develop junior capability. A company cannot complain about lack of ready-made talent while dismantling the pathways that create it. A workforce system cannot rely indefinitely on other organizations to train people first.

This is not only a hiring issue.

It is a capability-regeneration issue.

The wrong metric asks:

**Can this person perform immediately with minimal support?**

The better question is:

**What capability can this person develop, and what system would allow that development to happen responsibly?**

That does not mean lowering standards.

It means recognizing that capability is not only found.

It is built.

## AI Makes the Metric Problem Sharper

The World Economic Forum’s Chief People Officers Outlook describes low vacancy rates, shifting worker expectations, and rapid AI adoption as forces reshaping how organizations plan, hire, and design work.[^wef-cpo-outlook] Inc’s summary of that report emphasizes AI deployment, role redesign, upskilling, human-centered implementation, and concern about skill atrophy.[^inc-hiring-stalled]

That matters because AI can intensify both sides of the metric problem.

Used well, AI can help organize evidence, surface patterns, support review, identify missing information, and assist humans in making better decisions.

Used poorly, AI can help organizations process more people without understanding them better. It can create cleaner summaries of weak evidence, rank candidates based on inherited assumptions, make a flawed process look more sophisticated, and reduce visible workload while increasing invisible loss.

The question is not whether AI belongs in hiring or workforce systems.

The question is what the AI is optimizing.

If it optimizes for volume reduction, speed, defensibility, similarity to prior hires, keyword resemblance, or risk avoidance, it may intensify the same failures already present in the system.

If it helps humans understand evidence of capability more clearly, it may support better judgment.

That distinction is not technical decoration.

It is the center of the problem.

SHRM’s AI+HI discussion frames the same issue from inside HR: speed alone is not the goal. The challenge is activating AI in ways that improve quality, reduce bias, expand access to opportunity, and preserve human judgment. That framing matters because it shifts the question away from whether AI is present and toward what outcome the system is designed to produce.[^shrm-ai-hi]

## What OSI Measures

Organizational Systems Intelligence, or OSI, starts from the organizational side of this failure.

OSI asks where capability is created, lost, misread, blocked, retained, or deployed.

In hiring, that means looking beyond the number of applicants, interviews, rejections, and hires. It means asking what the system actually does to human capability evidence as it moves through the organization.

Where does evidence enter? Where is it translated, flattened, filtered, or ignored? Where does uncertainty disappear? Where do proxy signals become decisions? Where does human judgment happen, and where is it prevented from happening?

That is the OSI lens.

A hiring system is not merely an HR workflow.

It is a capability-recognition system.

If the wrong metrics steer it, the organization may not simply hire poorly.

It may lose access to the people it needs.

## What PIA/PCA Gives the Person

Professional Identity Architecture and Professional Capability Architecture approach the same problem from the person’s side.

The current system asks people to compress complex human development into a résumé, then hopes the machine and the organization interpret it correctly.

That is not enough.

A person’s capability may be distributed across roles, projects, artifacts, crises, tools, training, informal leadership, self-directed learning, and cross-domain transfer. One résumé cannot carry that whole structure.

PIA/PCA points toward a different model:

**The future is not the perfect résumé. The future is a personally owned coherent evidence system.**

That evidence system can produce a targeted résumé, but it does not collapse into the résumé. It can also support a capability brief, evidence map, interview proof pack, portfolio summary, learning and development map, and role-specific capability crosswalk.

The point is not to fake fit.

The point is to translate real fit.

## The Missing Layer

Organizations have applicant tracking systems.

Candidates have résumés.

What is missing is the layer between messy human capability and formal organizational recognition.

That layer needs to preserve distinctions the current system often collapses: evidence is not interpretation, interpretation is not proof, a proxy is not the person, a score is not judgment, a formatted résumé is not capability, and a rejected application is not proof that capability was absent.

The missing layer is a capability-recognition and evidence-translation layer.

On the person side, that means a portable, personally owned evidence system.

On the organization side, that means a reader layer that can receive structured capability evidence without treating formatting artifacts, keyword gaps, or nonlinear career histories as absence of qualification.

That does not replace human judgment.

It gives human judgment better material to work with.

## Better Metrics

If wrong metrics are steering the system, better metrics should ask different questions.

Not only how fast the role was filled, how many applicants were rejected, how many candidates matched the posting, or how much recruiter time was saved.

But what capability the organization failed to see. Which requirements were evidence-based, and which were inherited proxies. Which candidates were rejected before meaningful review. Which nontraditional evidence patterns were ignored. Where candidate evidence became unreadable. Where AI assisted judgment, and where it replaced contact.

The better question is not only whether the process was efficient.

It is whether real capability became visible, reviewable, and usable before the organization lost it.

Those questions are harder.

They are also closer to the real problem.

## Conclusion

The hiring system does not only need more speed.

It needs better aim.

A system optimized for volume control, defensibility, and administrative efficiency may still fail to recognize capability.

A company can say it cannot find talent while filtering out people it never learned to understand. A labor market can appear scarce because recognition systems are broken. A workforce can appear underqualified because organizations stopped building development pathways. A hiring process can look efficient while quietly losing access to the people who could have helped.

That is why the talent-scarcity narrative is incomplete.

The deeper issue is capability access failure.

Article 4 in this series focused on hidden workers and capability leakage: the people and capability patterns organizations fail to see.

This article argues that leakage is not accidental. It is often produced by the metrics steering the system.

The wrong metrics reward speed without understanding, consistency without interpretation, defensibility without judgment, and volume reduction without capability access.

A better system would measure something harder and more important:

whether real human capability can become visible, reviewable, and usable before the organization loses it.
