# OSI Hippocratic Principle

## Status

Foundational governance principle.

## Principle

Organizational Systems Intelligence shall be designed and used to
support understanding, healing, development, and responsible human
attention.

It shall not be designed or used primarily to control, exploit,
dehumanize, or punish the people whose activity makes the organization
possible.

---

## Diagnostic, not coercive

OSI should function more like a medical diagnostic than a system of
behavioral control.

A diagnostic identifies patterns that may require attention.

It does not replace:

- judgment;
- dialogue;
- context;
- consent;
- leadership;
- care;
- or human responsibility.

OSI findings should guide inquiry, not automatically determine guilt,
punishment, employment action, or individual worth.

---

## Human beings are not merely data sources

People represented within OSI are participants in a cooperative human
system.

They must not be reduced to:

- productivity scores;
- risk scores;
- sentiment values;
- compliance ratings;
- or machine-generated judgments.

Data about people is incomplete, contextual, and potentially harmful
when separated from human meaning.

---

## Minimum necessary observation

OSI should use the minimum amount of data necessary to answer a
legitimate organizational question.

The existence of accessible data does not create an ethical right to
collect, retain, combine, or analyze it.

---

## Appropriate human attention

OSI should help identify locations where responsible human attention
may be needed.

Examples include:

- overloaded teams;
- blocked capability;
- communication failures;
- declining predictability;
- leadership gaps;
- unaddressed conflict;
- repeated harmful state transitions;
- and structural conditions that prevent people from succeeding.

The system should direct care and inquiry toward the condition rather
than automatically assigning blame to an individual.

---

## Protection against automated harm

No high-impact decision should be made solely from an OSI-generated
assessment.

Human review, contextual evidence, and a meaningful opportunity to
challenge or correct the interpretation must be present.

---

## Visibility and explainability

People affected by OSI should be able to understand, at an appropriate
level:

- what is being measured;
- why it is being measured;
- how the information is used;
- who may access it;
- what conclusions may be drawn;
- and how errors may be corrected.

---

## Scientific humility

OSI must clearly distinguish between:

- observation;
- inference;
- hypothesis;
- assessment;
- interpretation;
- and verified conclusion.

Uncertainty must not be hidden behind technical language or numerical
precision.

---

## Core constraint

The ability to measure a human system does not create the moral right
to control it.

OSI must remain a tool for understanding and responsible intervention,
not a mechanism for building a more efficient form of domination.
